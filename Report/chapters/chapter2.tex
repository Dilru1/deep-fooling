\subsubsection{RL Methodology and Architecture Choices}
The selection of Reinforcement Learning (RL) frameworks is governed by two fundamental dimensions of agent learning and interaction:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Environment Modeling (Model-Based vs. Model-Free):} Model-based RL attempts to internalize the environment's transition dynamics, whereas \textbf{Model-Free} methods (e.g., PPO, TRPO) learn optimal policies directly from experience. We adopt a model-free approach to bypass the computational complexity of modeling non-linear, stochastic aero-hydrodynamics involving wind-wave interactions.
    \item \textbf{Update Mechanism (On-Policy vs. Off-Policy):} \textbf{On-Policy} algorithms (PPO/TRPO) strictly utilize data generated by the current policy for optimization, ensuring stable convergence. While off-policy methods offer higher sample efficiency, our use of a vectorized parallel environment mitigates this gap, prioritizing the stable policy refinement necessary for complex CMG maximization.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Preliminaries: Policy Gradient Methods}
With the classification established, we now turn to the mathematical basis of the chosen approach.

\subsubsection{The Objective Function}
In a standard Reinforcement Learning setting modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), the agent interacts with an environment defined by states \(s \in \mathcal{S}\), actions \(a \in \mathcal{A}\), and a reward function \(r(s,a)\). The goal is to learn a policy \(\pi_\theta(a|s)\), parameterized by \(\theta\), that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward:
\[ J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t r_t \right] \]
where \(\tau = (s_0, a_0, r_0, s_1, \dots)\) represents a trajectory, and \(\gamma \in [0, 1]\) is the discount factor that determines the importance of future rewards.

\subsubsection{The Policy Gradient Theorem}
To optimize the objective function, we employ the Policy Gradient Theorem, which computes gradients directly with respect to policy parameters. However, standard "vanilla" methods (e.g., REINFORCE) are notoriously unstable due to their sensitivity to the learning rate step size. A step too large causes performance collapse, while a step too small results in slow convergence. This instability necessitates the use of more robust Trust Region methods.

\subsection{From TRPO to PPO}
To address the instability of vanilla policy gradients, where large step sizes can cause performance collapse, Schulman et al. (2015) introduced \textbf{Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)}. TRPO guarantees monotonic improvement by enforcing a "Trust Region" constraint, ensuring the new policy $\pi_\theta$ does not deviate significantly from the old one $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$. This is formalized by constraining the KL divergence:
\[ \mathbb{E} \left[ KL[\pi_{\theta_{old}}(\cdot|s) || \pi_\theta(\cdot|s)] \right] \le \delta \]

However, TRPO is computationally expensive as it requires second-order optimization (Hessian matrix calculations). This motivated the development of \textbf{Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)} (Schulman et al., 2017), which simplifies the trust region concept into a first-order objective. PPO uses a \textit{Clipped Surrogate Objective} to pessimistically bound policy updates:
\[ L^{CLIP}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \min(r_t(\theta) \hat{A}_t, \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \hat{A}_t) \right] \]
where $r_t(\theta)$ is the probability ratio and $\epsilon$ is a clipping parameter (e.g., 0.2). This objective effectively mimics the stability benefits of TRPO while being computationally efficient. Finally, to reduce variance in gradient estimates, both algorithms typically utilize \textit{Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE)} to compute the advantage $\hat{A}_t$.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Settings}
We selected specific parameters to match the boat's physics and the noisy wind environment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Discount Factor ($\gamma = 0.995$):} 
    The boat has high inertia, meaning it takes time to react to the rudder. We use a high $\gamma$ (standard is 0.99) to make the agent "look ahead" and plan for delayed effects, rather than just reacting to the immediate moment.
    
    \item \textbf{GAE Parameter ($\lambda = 0.92$):} 
    The wind and waves create a lot of random noise. We lowered $\lambda$ (standard is 0.95) to help the agent ignore these short-term fluctuations and focus on the overall smooth trajectory.

    \item \textbf{Entropy Coefficient ($0.01$):} 
    To prevent the boat from getting stuck in a "safe" but slow state (like staying head-to-wind), this small value forces the agent to keep trying slightly different rudder angles to discover faster routes (standard is 0.0).

    \item \textbf{Network Architecture ($64 \times 64$):} 
    We use a small, lightweight network instead of a large one. This reacts faster (ensuring our 2Hz control rate) and produces smoother rudder movements, as it doesn't try to "over-analyze" every small wave.

    \item \textbf{Rollout Horizon ($128$ steps):} 
    This equals about 60 seconds of sailing. It is long enough for the agent to complete a full maneuver (like a turn) and see the result before updating its strategy (standard is 2048).

    \item \textbf{Learning Rate (Linear Decay):} 
    Instead of the original 0.0003, we start with a high rate ($10^{-3}$) to quickly learn basic sailing, then gradually lower it to $10^{-5}$. This "slow down" allows the agent to fine-tune the rudder for maximum speed without destabilizing.
\end{itemize}
